The criticisms

1. The title overclaims, and the last line of the course makes it worse. ./vc prints "You built vLLM. Every stage passes." A student built a single-process, single-GPU engine for one model. vLLM V1 is a separate engine process with ZMQ, an executor layer, FlashAttention and FlashInfer backends, dozens of model architectures, LoRA, MoE, PP, EP, multi-node, and a scheduler that is cache-aware. Put a "what you did not build" table in the README and change that message. Nothing kills trust faster than a graduate who says "I built vLLM" in an interview and cannot name the parts that are missing.

2. One model hides most of the work. Everything runs on Qwen3-0.6B: 28 layers, GQA 16/8, head_dim 128, tied embeddings, standard RoPE. Production breaks on the shapes that your course never sees: MHA and MQA, head_dim 64 or 96 or 256, sliding-window attention, alternating local and global layers, MLA, MoE routing, untied lm_head, and RoPE scaling. Your kernels and your cache layout have constants that only one config proves. Add a second architecture (Llama-3.2-1B has head_dim 64) and parametrize the kernel tests over both. It costs one fixture and it finds real bugs.

3. The contexts are too short to show the problems that pay my salary. Your checks run at 128 to 2048 tokens, and the graph buckets go to 256. Every hard serving problem starts at 8k and above: the KV read passes the weight read, block tables get long, flash-decoding starts to matter, the prefix cache hit rate decides your capacity, and the FP8 KV win appears. Your own roofline argument flips sign there. A 0.6B model at 16k is cheap. Add one long-context stage, and make stage 28 report the numbers at 1k and at 16k.

4. The gates optimize the least interesting operating point. "At least 1.2x over eager at batch 1." "At least 1.3x at batch 1." Batch 1 is a demo. The reason vLLM exists is throughput under concurrency with continuous batching. Your headline number should be output tokens for each second at a fixed p99 TTFT and ITL, which is goodput, measured at batch 32 and 128. You teach goodput in Part 6 and then do not gate on it.

5. You teach the launch overhead, but not the architectural fix. CUDA graphs are in stage 23. Good. But V1 moved the engine into its own process because Python work (detokenization, HTTP, output handling) was stealing the GPU between steps. That is the largest engineering lesson of the last two years, and the course does not have it. Add a stage that moves the engine loop to a child process with a queue, and measure the step time under real HTTP load. Your stage 27 server runs the engine in the same event loop.

6. Your attention kernel is never compared with the state of the art. The student writes a paged kernel with split-K and gates at "3x over stage 07". Against FlashAttention-3 or FlashInfer with a paged cache, that kernel is likely 3x to 10x slower. Do not hide this. Add a print-only comparison. A student who knows the size of that gap is useful to me. A student who thinks their kernel is production quality is not.

7. The API surface is a toy, and support tickets live in what is missing. There is no logprobs, no n > 1, no min_p, no bad_words, no beam search, no tool calls, and the only structured output is JSON. n > 1 is the interesting one: it forces a fork of a sequence and copy-on-write, which your stage 09 builds and the engine then never uses. That is a gap in the course, not only in the API.

8. The prefix cache stops at the easy half. You build hashing, refcounts and copy-on-write. You do not build eviction under pressure with LRU over free blocks, and you do not make the scheduler cache-aware. In production the cache changes which request you admit first, and the hit rate decides your capacity. Measure hit rate against pool size, and show the failure mode where eviction and preemption fight.

9. Tensor parallelism is algebra, not engineering. Stage 20 simulates ranks on one GPU. The algebra is correct and the collective count is the right lesson. But the engineering is NCCL, the overlap of communication with compute, sequence parallelism for the norms, and reductions that are not deterministic. Say clearly in the stage that it teaches the shape and not the system. Offer an optional two-GPU path for students who have one.

10. Evaluation still has a hole, and your own data points at it. You now measure KL and top-1 agreement against bf16. That is a real improvement over perplexity. But you measured something more important and did not act on it: two bf16 runs that differ only in the order of additions disagree at about 2% of positions. That is the batch-invariance problem. Users report it as "the same prompt gives different text when the server is busy". Add a check: the same request, with the same seed, at batch 1 and at batch 32, must give the same tokens. If it cannot pass, say why, and make the reason a lesson. This is a current, open problem in the field, and your course is one of the few places that could teach it.

11. Speculative decoding sells a number that only a copy task can reach. n-gram drafting with 1.5x on a copy task is honest in the test, but a reader of the README sees "speculative decoding, lossless, inside the engine". Real deployments use a draft model, EAGLE or MTP, and the hard parts are batched speculation with a paged cache, KV rollback on rejection, and the interaction with chunked prefill and preemption. At minimum, state the limit next to the claim.

12. The pool size is set by hand. Your tests pass num_blocks as a constant. Real engines profile: run a dummy forward, measure the peak memory, take a fraction of what is free, and derive the block count. Students hit this on day one of a deployment, and it is 30 lines. Add it to stage 21 or 22.

13. Two tracks double the maintenance and dilute the point. The JAX track is elegant and the "shapes are frozen" lesson is real. But vLLM is torch. You now keep 92 notebooks and two ladders in step. If you must choose where to spend the next month, spend it on long context, concurrency and the process split, not on the second backend.

What I would do next, in order

1. Change the completion message and add the "what you did not build" table. Honesty first.
2. Add goodput at batch 32 and 128, and long context at 16k, to stage 28. These change what the whole c
3. Add the batch-invariance check and make it a lesson.
4. Add the second model architecture to the kernel and cache tests.
5. Add the engine process split, under HTTP load.
6. Add logprobs and n > 1, and use the copy-on-write that you already built.

The course is better than most of what exists. My criticism is not that it teaches the wrong things. Itt where the interesting failures start: many users, long contexts, several shapes, and a server thatmust not lie about its own numbers.

14. The Colab claim looks wrong, and it is the first thing a new person reads. The README says a free Colab T4 puts stages "8, 8b, 8c, 12, 18, 18b, 21-28" in reach. A T4 is sm_75. It has no bf16 tensor cores and no FP8 hardware. Your capstone loads the model in bf16 by default, and stage 24b needs float8_e4m3fn. Either that path fails, or it runs so slowly that every speed gate becomes noise. Run the capstone on a T4 one time, then rewrite the claim, or name the Colab tiers that work (L4, A100).

15. No LICENSE, no CI, no contribution path. There is no LICENSE, no CONTRIBUTING.md and no .github/. Without a license, nobody can legally fork your course, and a company cannot use it for onboarding. Without CI, "all 776 checks pass" is a statement about one laptop on one day. Add a CPU-only job for the pure-logic stages and the harness, and a GPU job for the rest.

16. The dependencies float. setup.sh installs torch and transformers>=4.51 with no pin. HuggingFace changed the cache API of generate() inside that range, and your stage 01, 02, 17 and 25 checks all compare against model.generate. A student who starts this course in six months may hit failures that have nothing to do with their code. Pin exact versions, and test one newer version on purpose.

17. The benchmark workload is not a serving trace. runner/bench.py sends random token ids with three prompt lengths and three output lengths. Real traces are heavy-tailed, and lengths correlate with the content. Random ids also make the model produce unnatural distributions, which changes when EOS arrives and what a draft accepts. Your own Part 4 notebooks already use lognormal lengths. Use them here, or a ShareGPT-style trace.

18. Your best feature never appears in your final number. The benchmark prompts are random, so no two requests share a prefix. The prefix cache, the feature that saves the most money in production (system prompts and multi-turn chat), contributes nothing to stage 28. Add a workload with a shared system prompt and report the hit rate next to the tokens for each second.

19. There is no multi-turn conversation anywhere. The server has /v1/chat/completions, and the checks send one turn. Real chat sends turn 1, then turn 1 plus turn 2, and so on. That workload is the reason prefix caching, block reuse and cache-aware scheduling exist. One test with three turns would show a student what the cache does.

20. The failure paths are missing. I see no handling for: a step that raises CUDA OOM, a queue with no limit, a request timeout, a graceful shutdown with requests in flight, or a watchdog for a hung step. In production these decide if a bad request takes down the node or only itself. One stage on backpressure and recovery teaches more than one more kernel.

21. Nothing guards against NaN. A bad quantization scale, a bad FP8 calibration or an overflow in attention gives NaN logits. Your sampler then samples garbage, and the user sees fluent nonsense with no error. Add an assert on the logits of each step, and make the failure a lesson: NaN is silent, and only a check makes it loud.

22. The server has no limits. There is no cap on the prompt length at the HTTP layer, no cap on max_tokens, and no limit on concurrent requests. One client can fill the whole block pool. Even for teaching code, that is the first thing a reviewer asks about a server.

23. The measurements report one number, not a spread. You gate on ratios, which is correct, and you interleave the rounds for the kernel gates, which is also correct. But a laptop GPU throttles, and a student who sees 1.19x once has no way to know if that is noise or a real fail. Print the median and the spread of three runs, and warn when the clocks drop during a measurement.

24. The course never opens the real vLLM source. A student finishes 32 stages named after vLLM and has not read vllm/v1/core/sched/scheduler.py or the GPU model runner. The strongest last stage is not another benchmark. It is: read the upstream scheduler, map it to the class that you wrote, list the five things upstream does that you do not, and explain why. Then send one small patch. That is how a course about my project should end.

25. The name needs attribution. The course is "Build Your Own vLLM" and uses the project name throughout. State that it is not affiliated with the vLLM project, link the upstream repo and its license, and say which version of the design you teach. V1 changed the architecture, and a course frozen on old ideas confuses new contributors.

26. There is no time budget and no hardware budget. Nothing tells a student that the course needs, for example, 40 to 60 hours, a GPU with nvcc, about 10 GB of VRAM and a few GB of disk for the weights. A person who starts and stops at stage 12 because they ran out of GPU credit is a failure of the README, not of the student.
